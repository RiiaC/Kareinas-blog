# seminar
Today's Archaeology Seminar was on the *Transmission of Iron Technology among Early Iron Age Hunter-Gatherers in Northern Fennoscandia* by Carina Bennerhag at Luleå University of Technoloy/Norrbottens Museum. Keldor and I heard Carina talk in [[2025-10-15 that's a nope|October of 2025]], but it is such an interesting topic, I couldn't resist listening again. Since Keldor had to work, he called me just as the lecture started, and listened in as he kept working. I took photos, of the slides so he can see them later. I apologize for the quality of many of these photos. I was trying to be descrete, but perhaps I was too discrete, as the phone clearly didn't always have time to actually focus. Oops. ![[2026-11-05_seminar-1.jpg|500]]

This research stems, in a large part from Carina's doctoral research, and is being continued as a postdoctoral researcher, doing even more analysis, and even delving into historical records
![[2026-11-05_seminar-2.jpg|500]]

This research challenges the traditional viewpoint that metal working started in the south and only gradually spread outwards from there. Indeed, most of those traditional "spread of iron" maps like this (blurry, sorry), one don't even extend all the way to northern Sweden, Finland, and Norway.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-3.jpg|500]]

But the excavations in Sangis, starting a decade ago, kicked of a chain of research that started challenging those old beliefs, as they found an iron furnace.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-4.jpg|500]]

In 2017 Carina was part of another excavation, of another iron furnace, even further north.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-5.jpg|500]]

This made them wonder if, perhaps, there were more. So they went poking around in old collections, from the excavations that had been done in the middle of the 1900's, when Sweden was putting in hydro electric power, and they needed to rescue artefacts fast, before the dams filled the valleys and covered them with water. It turns out that many of those old boxes contained slag from iron making, but the archaologists all those decades ago hadn't really thought about it.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-6.jpg|500]]

Once they started looking, they found ever so many more. Look at the difference between the known iron making sites ten years ago, and those we know about today!
![[2026-11-05_seminar-7.jpg|500]]

Thanks to C14 dating, we now know that the earliest iron (steel) production up here dates at least as far back as ~700-500 BC.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-8.jpg|500]]

And became very wide spread during the centuries before and after the start of first millennium. 
![[2026-11-05_seminar-9.jpg|500]]

Then Carina gave an introduction to how the early shaft furnaces work to make iron from limonite ore, producing iron in a solid state. While slag does form, and sink to the bottom of the pit, the iron part of the ore stays (mostly) solid as it transforms.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-10.jpg|500]]

Carina has participated in some experiments to build the same sort of clay shaft furnaces for making iron
![[2026-11-05_seminar-11.jpg|500]]

Here is a photo of the base of one of these furnaces, built of flat stones  to line the slag pit. This one had been in use around 100 BC.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-12.jpg|500]]

This sort has been found all over northern Sweden, Finland, and even some in southern Sweden
![[2026-11-05_seminar-13.jpg|500]]

They have analysed the clay fragments from the furnace walls from some of the sites. They document the very high heat used to make the steel, and even show layers where the furnace walls had been repaired with new layers of clay in between use, and then the furnace was re-fired. Some fragments, like this one, document more than one cycle of repair and reuse.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-14.jpg|500]]

They have also documentation differences in what sorts of clay were used in different places, and how those differences effected the furnace quality. One as yet unanswered question is why the two analysed from Norway used clay of such poor quality for the job? Was there nothing better in that area? or was it only ever intended to be a single use thing? Even so, the cracks  may well have let too much oxygen into the system for a truly good result.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-15.jpg|500]]

Alas, the worst photo had the most interesting pictures. one of the photos appears in the [[2025-10-15 that's a nope#Quality steel|photos from last year]], showing some of the steel fragments they have analysed
![[2026-11-05_seminar-16.jpg|500]]

They have also analysed some of the steel artefacts from the region
![[2026-11-05_seminar-17.jpg|500]]

Which are high quality steel, with the harder steel in the middle (to take a nice cutting edge), and softer steel on the outside (to keep the knife from being too brittle)
![[2026-11-05_seminar-19.jpg|500]]

More pretty steel analysis photos
![[2026-11-05_seminar-20.jpg|500]]

The various excavations reveal two different sorts of slag cake. The A type has no slag attached to the clay furnace lining, nor yet any clay attached to the slag itself.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-21.jpg|500]]

The B-type on the other hand, shows clay sticking to the slag, and vice versa. (they don't yet know why the difference)
![[2026-11-05_seminar-22.jpg|500]]

Making iron is not an easy task--it requires a community, working together, for an extended period, to accomplish all of the preparation steps before running the furnace itself.
![[2026-11-05_seminar-23.jpg|500]]

Some of the furnace sites were in the same general area as where the smiths did their work to transform the steel into knives etc. (about half a kilometer between the smithing and the iron production sites in Sangis, while one of the other sites there was only about 30 meters between the two)
![[2026-11-05_seminar-24.jpg|500]]

Excavations show that other crafts were also produced near the settlement sites associated with Iron production
![[2026-11-05_seminar-25.jpg|500]]

So far, all of the iron production sites seem to be associated with large lake systems, that are both a source of ore, but also a source of fish
![[2026-11-05_seminar-26.jpg|500]]

Indeed, doing a second sifting of the soils from the excavations revealed many, many small fish bones. Clearly the hunter-gatherer people living in this region and production iron relied on fish to a great extent. 

After the main talk (and the slides) were done, the question and answer period went into a discussion on hunting and gathering, and how archaeologists used to assume that making steel was something that settled farming cultures did, and that hunter and gatherers who used iron tools traded for them. But, clearly, at least in the north, the hunter gatherers were making their own steel.

It is not yet known to what extent, if any, they were trading it away, or if they were only making it for thier own needs. Nor is it clearly known to what extent these people lived in one place, nor for how long at a time. Iron making is a summer season activity, so that they did it could point to a more sedentary summer existence than had previously been assumed. Further research is required.
